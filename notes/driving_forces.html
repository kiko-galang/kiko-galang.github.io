Origin of Driving Forces from Fundamentals

Entropy Production, Curtiss–Bird Driving Forces, and the Onsager Framework

Motivation

Many continuum treatments of multicomponent transport identify the diffusion “driving force” with a chemical-potential gradient, often written schematically as \(c_i\nabla\mu_i\) (or an equivalent normalized form). This perspective appears across electrolyte and porous-media modeling frameworks — in Crothers, Fong, Newman, and Weber — and is commonly traced back to the nonequilibrium-thermodynamic development of Hirschfelder–Curtiss–Bird and Curtiss–Bird. The algebra leading from the local entropy balance to a concrete multicomponent form is often unclear, which can make its use difficult without awareness of the underlying assumptions.

This note derives the driving-force density following the work of Curtiss and Bird, starting from the local entropy balance and making each rearrangement explicit. The immediate goal is to clarify the most general origin of diffusion driving forces.

In particular, we:

  1. Start from the Gibbs relation and the conservation laws to obtain the entropy production \(\sigma\);
  2. Identify the diffusive contribution and define a generalized driving force \(\mathbf{d}_i\);
  3. Define the corresponding force density \(\mathbf{F}_i = cRT\mathbf{d}_i\).

Part 1: Entropy Production and the Derivation of Driving Forces

We follow the development in Molecular Theory of Gases and Liquids (Hirschfelder, Curtiss, Bird) and Multicomponent Diffusion (Curtiss, Bird), assuming the reader is comfortable with the standard continuum balance laws and basic thermodynamics. Transport in a given direction is a spontaneous microscopic process that dissipates free energy and produces entropy; under local equilibrium, the macroscopic dynamics can be described by thermodynamic forces and their conjugate fluxes.

The Fundamental Entropy Balance

Goal: State the general entropy balance and determine the entropy production \(\sigma\).

We start from the local entropy balance in divergence form:

\[ \rho\frac{D\hat{S}}{Dt} = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{s} + g_S \tag{1}\]

where \(\mathbf{s}\) is the entropy flux and \(g_S \ge 0\) is the local entropy production. Here \(D/Dt\) is the substantial derivative:

\[ \frac{D(\cdot)}{Dt} = \frac{\partial(\cdot)}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla(\cdot) \]

We combine (i) a Gibbs relation for a multicomponent mixture with (ii) the conservation laws. Starting from the equilibrium Gibbs relation per unit mass:

\[ d\hat{U} = T\,d\hat{S} - p\,d\hat{V} + \sum_{i=1}^v \frac{\mu_i}{M_i}\,d\omega_i \tag{2}\]

Taking the substantial derivative and rearranging:

\[ T \frac{D\hat{S}}{Dt} = \frac{D\hat{U}}{Dt} + p\,\frac{D(1/\rho)}{Dt} - \sum_{i=1}^v \frac{\mu_i}{M_i}\,\frac{D\omega_i}{Dt} \tag{3}\]

Step 1.1: Substitute Conservation Laws

We replace the derivatives using the conservation equations.

A. Conservation of energy (MTGL Eq. 11.1-4):

\[ \rho\frac{D\hat{U}}{Dt} = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{q} - p\,(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}) - (\boldsymbol{\tau}:\nabla\mathbf{v}) + \sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i\cdot\mathbf{g}_i \tag{4}\]

B. Continuity equation (MTGL Eq. 11.1-2):

\[ \frac{D(1/\rho)}{Dt} = -\frac{1}{\rho^2}\frac{D\rho}{Dt} = \frac{1}{\rho}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}) \tag{5}\]

C. Species continuity (MTGL Eq. 11.1-1), neglecting reactions (\(r_i = 0\)):

\[ \rho\frac{D\omega_i}{Dt} = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}_i \tag{6}\]

Step 1.2: The Substitution

Multiply Equation 3 by \(\rho\) and substitute Equation 4–Equation 6. The \(p(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})\) terms cancel, giving:

\[ \rho\frac{D\hat{S}}{Dt} = -\frac{1}{T}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{q} -\frac{1}{T}(\boldsymbol{\tau}:\nabla\mathbf{v}) + \frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i\cdot\mathbf{g}_i + \sum_{i=1}^v \frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}_i \tag{7}\]

Step 1.3: Isolate Divergences

Using \(\nabla\cdot(A\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}\cdot\nabla A + A\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{x}\):

\[ -\frac{1}{T}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{q} = -\nabla\cdot\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}}{T}\right) - \frac{\mathbf{q}\cdot\nabla T}{T^2} \]

\[ \frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}_i = \nabla\cdot\!\left(\frac{\mu_i\mathbf{j}_i}{M_i T}\right) - \mathbf{j}_i\cdot\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\right) \]

Substituting back gives the entropy balance in the form Equation 1, with the entropy production:

\[ \boxed{ g_S = -\frac{\mathbf{q}\cdot\nabla T}{T^2} -\frac{1}{T}(\boldsymbol{\tau}:\nabla\mathbf{v}) - \sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i\cdot\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\right) + \sum_{i=1}^v \frac{\mathbf{j}_i\cdot\mathbf{g}_i}{T} } \tag{8}\]

Connection to Phenomenological (Flux–Force) Laws

Thermodynamics requires \(g_S \ge 0\). To guarantee positivity, one postulates linear phenomenological laws: each flux is proportional to its corresponding thermodynamic driving force.

General Structure: Flux \(\times\) Force

From Equation 8, the entropy production is a sum of products:

\[ g_S = \underbrace{-\frac{1}{T^2}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\nabla T)}_{\text{thermal}} \underbrace{-\frac{1}{T}(\boldsymbol{\tau}:\nabla\mathbf{v})}_{\text{viscous}} \underbrace{-\sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i\cdot\left[\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\right) - \frac{\mathbf{g}_i}{T}\right]}_{\text{diffusive}} \tag{9}\]

Written abstractly as \(\sigma = \sum_k \mathbf{J}_k\cdot\mathbf{X}_k \ge 0\), where \(\mathbf{J}_k\) is a flux and \(\mathbf{X}_k\) its conjugate thermodynamic force.

Identifying the Thermodynamic Forces

Heat conduction. Using \(\nabla(1/T) = -\nabla T/T^2\):

\[ -\frac{1}{T^2}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\nabla T) = \mathbf{q}\cdot\nabla\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\right) \]

Flux–force pair: \(\mathbf{J}_q = \mathbf{q}\), \(\mathbf{X}_q = \nabla(1/T)\). Temperature gradients produce entropy through heat conduction.

Viscous dissipation. \(\mathbf{J}_\mathrm{visc} = \boldsymbol{\tau}\), \(\mathbf{X}_\mathrm{visc} = -(1/T)\nabla\mathbf{v}\). Velocity gradients produce entropy.

Diffusion. The diffusive flux–force pair is \(\mathbf{J}_i = \mathbf{j}_i\) and:

\[ \mathbf{X}_i = -\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\right) + \frac{\mathbf{g}_i}{T} \]

Chemical-potential gradients and body forces produce entropy through mass transfer.

Summary of Flux–Force Pairs

Phenomenon Flux \(\mathbf{J}\) Force \(\mathbf{X}\)
Heat conduction \(\mathbf{q}\) \(\nabla(1/T)\)
Viscous dissipation \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) \(-(1/T)\nabla\mathbf{v}\)
Diffusion (species \(i\)) \(\mathbf{j}_i\) \(-\nabla(\mu_i/M_i T) + \mathbf{g}_i/T\)

Onsager Relations and Cross-Effects

To ensure \(g_S \ge 0\), assume linear flux–force relations:

\[ \mathbf{J}_a = \sum_b L_{ab}\,\mathbf{X}_b \tag{10}\]

with a positive-semidefinite coefficient matrix \(\mathbf{L}\). Off-diagonal terms capture the Soret effect (temperature gradient driving mass flux) and the Dufour effect (mass flux driving heat flux).

Part 2: Defining the Generalized Driving Force

Goal: Transform the raw chemical-potential gradient into the force-density form used by Crothers, Delacourt, and Weber.

The diffusive entropy production is:

\[ g_S^{\mathrm{diff}} = - \sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i\cdot\left[\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\right) - \frac{\mathbf{g}_i}{T}\right] \tag{11}\]

Since \(\sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i = \mathbf{0}\), there are only \(v-1\) independent diffusive mass fluxes. The conjugate thermodynamic driving forces are similarly constrained by the Gibbs–Duhem equation.

Step 2.1: Deriving the Curtiss–Bird Driving Force \(d_i\)

We start from the Gibbs–Duhem equation in the entropy representation:

\[ U\,d\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\right) + V\,d\!\left(\frac{p}{T}\right) - \sum_{i=1}^v N_i\,d\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{T}\right) = 0 \tag{12}\]

Step 2.1a: Divide by volume. Divide by \(V\) and define \(u := U/V\), \(c_i := N_i/V\):

\[ u\,d\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\right) + d\!\left(\frac{p}{T}\right) - \sum_{i=1}^v c_i\,d\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{T}\right) = 0 \]

Step 2.1b: Replace differentials by spatial gradients (local equilibrium):

\[ u\,\nabla\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\right) + \nabla\!\left(\frac{p}{T}\right) - \sum_{i=1}^v c_i\,\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{T}\right) = \mathbf{0} \]

Expanding the pressure term using the product rule:

\[ \nabla\!\left(\frac{p}{T}\right) = \frac{1}{T}\,\nabla p + p\,\nabla\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\right) \]

Substituting:

\[ (u+p)\,\nabla\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\right) + \frac{1}{T}\,\nabla p - \sum_{i=1}^v c_i\,\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{T}\right) = \mathbf{0} \tag{13}\]

Step 2.1c: Replace \((u+p)\) by enthalpy density. Since \(h = u+p = \sum_{i=1}^v c_i\,\bar{H}_i\):

\[ \sum_{i=1}^v c_i\,\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{T}\right) - \frac{1}{T}\,\nabla p - \sum_{i=1}^v c_i\,\bar{H}_i\,\nabla\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\right) = \mathbf{0} \]

Step 2.1d: Convert \(\nabla(1/T)\) to \(\nabla T\). Using \(\nabla(1/T) = -\nabla T/T^2\):

\[ \sum_{i=1}^v c_i\,\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{T}\right) - \frac{1}{T}\,\nabla p + \sum_{i=1}^v c_i\,\frac{\bar{H}_i}{T^2}\,\nabla T = \mathbf{0} \tag{14}\]

Step 2.1e: Replace \(c_i = \rho_i/M_i\) (Curtiss–Bird Eq. 1.4):

\[ \sum_{i=1}^v \rho_i\,\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\right) - \frac{1}{T}\,\nabla p + \sum_{i=1}^v \rho_i\,\frac{\bar{H}_i}{M_i T^2}\,\nabla T = \mathbf{0} \tag{15}\]

Step 2.1f: Decouple heat and mass transfer contributions.

Since \(\sum_i \rho_i/\rho = 1\), we write Equation 15 term-by-term:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^v \left[ \rho_i\,\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\right) - \frac{\rho_i}{\rho}\,\frac{1}{T}\,\nabla p + \rho_i\,\frac{\bar{H}_i}{M_i T^2}\,\nabla T \right] = \mathbf{0} \tag{16}\]

Ignoring viscous dissipation and adding the pressure and enthalpy terms inside the species summation so the Gibbs–Duhem constraint can be invoked:

\[ g_S = -\frac{1}{T^2}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\nabla T) - \sum_{i=1}^v \frac{\mathbf{j}_i}{\rho_i}\cdot\Bigg[ \underbrace{\rho_i\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\right) - \frac{\rho_i}{\rho}\,\frac{1}{T}\,\nabla p + \rho_i\frac{\bar{H}_i}{M_i T^2}\,\nabla T}_{\text{(A) constrained by Gibbs--Duhem} = 0} \;\underbrace{-\rho_i\frac{\bar{H}_i}{M_i T^2}\,\nabla T}_{\text{(B) ``free'' term}} + \rho_i \frac{\mathbf{g}_i}{T} + \frac{1}{T}\frac{\rho_i}{\rho}\sum_{j=1}^v\rho_j\mathbf{g}_j\Bigg] \tag{17}\]

Term (A) equals zero by the Gibbs–Duhem constraint Equation 16. The last two body-force terms also cancel (see Supplementary Material):

\[ g_S = -\frac{1}{T^2}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\nabla T) - \sum_{i=1}^v \frac{\mathbf{j}_i}{\rho_i}\cdot \left[ -\rho_i\frac{\bar{H}_i}{M_i T^2}\,\nabla T \right] \]

Heat-flux decomposition and cancellation of the enthalpy term.

Define the modified heat flux (Curtiss–Bird):

\[ \mathbf{q}^{(h)} := \mathbf{q} - \sum_{i=1}^v \frac{\bar{H}_i}{M_i}\,\mathbf{j}_i \tag{18}\]

Substituting \(\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{q}^{(h)} + \sum_i (\bar{H}_i/M_i)\mathbf{j}_i\) into the thermal contribution, the enthalpy terms cancel exactly, and we recover the full entropy production with the modified heat flux:

\[ g_S = -\frac{1}{T^2}(\mathbf{q}^{(h)}\cdot\nabla T) - \sum_{i=1}^v \frac{\mathbf{j}_i}{\rho_i}\cdot\Bigg[ \rho_i\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\right) - \frac{\rho_i}{\rho}\,\frac{1}{T}\,\nabla p + \rho_i\frac{\bar{H}_i}{M_i T^2}\,\nabla T + \rho_i\,\frac{\mathbf{g}_i}{T} + \frac{1}{T}\,\frac{\rho_i}{\rho}\sum_{j=1}^v \rho_j\mathbf{g}_j\Bigg] \]

Step 2.1g: Define the generalized driving force \(d_i\).

Focus on the diffusion entropy production. Following Curtiss–Bird, introduce driving forces \(d_i\) (units: reciprocal length) via the normalization \((cRT/\rho_i)d_i\):

\[ \boxed{ \frac{cRT}{\rho_i} d_i = T\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\right) + \frac{\bar{H}_i}{M_i}\nabla\ln T - \frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p - \mathbf{g}_i + \frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{j=1}^v \rho_j\mathbf{g}_j } \tag{19}\]

Step 2.2: Convert to Force-Density Units

Define \(\mathbf{F}_i := cRT\,d_i\), so multiplying Equation 19 by \(\rho_i\):

\[ \mathbf{F}_i = \rho_i T\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\right) + \rho_i\frac{\bar{H}_i}{M_i}\nabla\ln T - \omega_i\nabla p - \rho_i\mathbf{g}_i + \omega_i\sum_{j=1}^v \rho_j\mathbf{g}_j \tag{20}\]

Step 2.2a: Substitute mass-to-molar relations. Use \(\rho_i = c_i M_i\) and \(g_i = X_i/M_i\) (where \(X_i\) is force per mole).

Step 2.2b: Evaluate each term:

\[ \rho_i T\nabla\!\left(\frac{\mu_i}{M_i T}\right) = c_i\!\left(\nabla\mu_i - \frac{\mu_i}{T}\nabla T\right) \]

\[ \rho_i\frac{\bar{H}_i}{M_i}\nabla\ln T = c_i\,\frac{\bar{H}_i}{T}\,\nabla T \]

Step 2.2c: Assemble and factor:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{F}_i = c_i\!\left(\nabla\mu_i + \frac{\bar{H}_i-\mu_i}{T}\,\nabla T - \frac{M_i}{\rho}\nabla p - X_i + \frac{M_i}{\rho}\sum_{j=1}^v c_j X_j\right) } \tag{21}\]

Part 3: From Driving Force to the Curtiss–Bird Flux Law

Goal: Apply Onsager’s phenomenological framework to obtain the Curtiss–Bird constitutive flux equation.

Step 3.1: Write the Dissipation Function

Multiply the entropy production by \(T\) to form the dissipation function \(\Phi := Tg_S\). Substituting the definition of \(d_i\):

\[ \Phi = -\mathbf{q}^{(h)}\cdot\nabla\ln T \;-\; \sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i\cdot\frac{cRT}{\rho_i}\,d_i \tag{22}\]

Define shorthand forces:

\[ L_0 := \nabla\ln T,\qquad L_i := \frac{cRT}{\rho_i}\,d_i \]

Step 3.2: Count the Independent Flux–Force Pairs

Flux side. \(\sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i = \mathbf{0}\) leaves \(v-1\) independent diffusion fluxes.

Force side. \(\sum_{i=1}^v \rho_i\,d_i = 0\) by Gibbs–Duhem, leaving \(v-1\) independent forces.

Removing redundancy. Since \(\sum_i \mathbf{j}_i = \mathbf{0}\), we may subtract any species-independent vector from each \(L_i\):

\[ \sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i\cdot L_i = \sum_{i\neq k} \mathbf{j}_i\cdot (L_i - L_k) \]

Step 3.3: Apply Onsager Linear Phenomenological Laws

\[ \mathbf{q}^{(h)} = -R_{00}\,L_0 - \sum_{j\neq k} R_{0j}\,(L_j - L_k) \tag{23}\]

\[ \mathbf{j}_i = -R_{i0}\,L_0 - \sum_{j\neq k} R_{ij}\,(L_j - L_k),\qquad i\neq k \tag{24}\]

with Onsager reciprocal relations \(R_{ij} = R_{ji}\).

Physical meaning of off-diagonal terms:

Coefficient Effect
\(R_{00}\) Thermal conduction
\(R_{ii}\) Self-diffusion
\(R_{i0} = R_{0i}\) Soret effect (thermal → mass flux)
\(R_{0j} = R_{j0}\) Dufour effect (mass → heat flux)
\(R_{ij},\; i\neq j\) Cross-diffusion

Step 3.4: Extend the Summation to All Species

Summing Equation 24 over all \(i\neq k\) and requiring \(\sum_{i\neq k}\mathbf{j}_i = -\mathbf{j}_k\):

\[ \sum_{i=1}^v R_{ij} = 0 \qquad \text{for all } j \]

This defines \(R_{kj} := -\sum_{i\neq k} R_{ij}\), allowing the sum to extend to all species:

\[ \sum_{j\neq k} R_{ij}(L_j - L_k) = \sum_{j=1}^v R_{ij}L_j \]

Result:

\[ \mathbf{j}_i = -R_{i0}\,\nabla\ln T \;-\; \sum_{j=1}^v R_{ij}\,\frac{cRT}{\rho_j}\,d_j, \qquad i=1,\ldots,v \tag{25}\]

Step 3.5: Identify Physical Transport Coefficients

Thermal diffusion coefficient:

\[ D_i^T := R_{i0} \qquad [\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}] \]

With \(\sum_i D_i^T = 0\) (only \(v-1\) independent).

Symmetric multicomponent diffusivity:

\[ \tilde{D}_{ij} := \frac{cRT\,R_{ij}}{\rho_i\rho_j} \qquad [\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}] \]

Onsager symmetry gives \(\tilde{D}_{ij} = \tilde{D}_{ji}\) immediately.

Step 3.6: The Curtiss–Bird Constitutive Flux Equation

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{j}_i = -D_i^T\,\nabla\ln T \;-\; \rho_i\!\sum_{j=1}^v \tilde{D}_{ij}\,d_j } \tag{26}\]

Reading this equation:

  • First term \((-D_i^T\,\nabla\ln T)\): the Soret (thermal diffusion) contribution. A temperature gradient alone drives a mass flux even at uniform composition.
  • Second term \((-\rho_i\sum_j \tilde{D}_{ij}\,d_j)\): the multicomponent diffusion contribution. The flux of species \(i\) depends on the driving forces of all species \(j\) through the symmetric matrix \(\tilde{D}_{ij}\).

Connection to Newman’s equation 13.1. The combination that emerges from substituting Equation 26 into the Stefan–Maxwell force balance is:

\[ A_j^i := \frac{D_j^T}{\rho_j} - \frac{D_i^T}{\rho_i} \]

which is precisely the quantity Newman defines. This confirms that Newman’s thermal diffusion correction is not an independent ingredient — it is the Stefan–Maxwell re-expression of the Curtiss–Bird Soret coefficient.

Summary

Equation 21 provides a generalized view of multicomponent diffusion driving forces: gradients in chemical potential, temperature (Soret effect), pressure (pressure diffusion), and differential body forces that act differently on different species (forced diffusion). In electrochemical applications, none of these terms can always be neglected. Examples include:

  • Thermogalvanic cells (temperature gradients)
  • Pressure-driven transport in PEM fuel cells
  • Sedimentation in semi-solid flow batteries
  • Centrifugal isotope separation

The “Newman approach” incorporates the electrostatic potential into the chemical potential to define the electrochemical potential, \(\bar{\mu}_i = \mu_i + z_i F\phi\), so the driving force becomes \(\nabla\bar{\mu}_i = \nabla\mu_i + z_iF\nabla\phi\). This is consistent with the generalized derivation above. More rigorous electrodynamic treatments (Fong and Mandadapu) recover this familiar form explicitly from first principles via the internal entropy production.

Connection to Stefan–Maxwell Framework

Under isothermal, isobaric conditions with no body forces, the Curtiss–Bird driving force leads naturally to the Stefan–Maxwell equations:

\[ c_i \nabla \mu_i = \sum_{j\neq i} K_{ij}\left(\mathbf{v}_j-\mathbf{v}_i\right) \tag{27}\]

This is a force balance: the thermodynamic force on species \(i\) (left) is balanced by frictional forces between species \(i\) and each other species. The Stefan–Maxwell coefficients relate to binary diffusivities as:

\[ K_{ij} = \frac{RT\,c_i c_j}{c_T D_{ij}} \]

Fuller and Newman show this can be inverted to obtain the Onsager flux form:

\[ N_i = -\sum_k \left(L_{ik}\,c_i c_k\,\nabla \mu_k\right) \]

These equivalences — driving-force form, Stefan–Maxwell, Onsager flux — will serve as the starting point for examining what assumptions allow reduction to Ohm’s law, Fick’s laws, and the Nernst–Planck equation.

Supplementary Material: The Body Force Terms

In Equation 17 we introduced a body-force term and stated it contributes zero. Here we show why explicitly.

The diffusive fluxes satisfy \(\sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i = \mathbf{0}\), so for any species-independent vector field \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}\):

\[ \sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i\cdot\boldsymbol{\Psi} = \boldsymbol{\Psi}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i = 0 \]

Define the mixture-average body force:

\[ \mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{mix}} := \sum_{j=1}^v \frac{\rho_j}{\rho}\,\mathbf{g}_j \]

Decompose \(\mathbf{g}_i\):

\[ \mathbf{g}_i = \big(\mathbf{g}_i-\mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{mix}}\big) + \mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{mix}} \]

Substituting into the body-force entropy production:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i\cdot\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{g}_i = \sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i\cdot\frac{1}{T}(\mathbf{g}_i-\mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{mix}}) + \underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\,\mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{mix}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^v \mathbf{j}_i}_{=\; 0} \]

Physical interpretation. A body force that accelerates all species equally (\(\mathbf{g}_i = \mathbf{g}\) for all \(i\)) produces \(\mathbf{g}_i - \mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{mix}} = \mathbf{0}\) and drives no diffusion. Only differential body forces (e.g., electric forces depending on charge, magnetic forces) contribute to the driving force — which is the physically correct result.

Expanding:

\[ \mathbf{g}_i - \mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{mix}} = \mathbf{g}_i - \frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{j=1}^v \rho_j\mathbf{g}_j \]

This replacement of \(\mathbf{g}_i\) by \(\mathbf{g}_i - \mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{mix}}\) inside the driving term produces the additional mixture-average correction:

\[ -\mathbf{g}_i \;\longrightarrow\; -\mathbf{g}_i + \frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{j=1}^v \rho_j\mathbf{g}_j \]

which is precisely the “extra” term appearing in Equation 19.
